Cryptographic Foundation & Notes

Elliptic-curve scalar multiplication is a cryptographic one-way function. It is deterministic and simple to compute in the forward direction (k → Q = k·G), while recovering the secret private key k from the public point Q is computationally infeasible (ECDLP). Here, we explore these group laws on a tractable finite field 𝔽₁₇.

For the study of elliptic curves, I used this document as my main source: RFC 6090
Statistical Analysis of Points Generated by Scalar Multiplication Click to expand

To understand how points behave on an elliptic curve we can study some basic statistical properties. These include the distribution of coordinates, the frequency of individual values, how bits are distributed, and any correlations between the input scalar and the coordinates of the resulting point. These analyses help us find possible patterns or anomalies in the observed sequence.

However, an apparently uniform distribution or the absence of clear correlations is not enough to prove cryptographic security. In scalar multiplication Q = kP, the essential property is that finding the scalar k from points P and Q is computationally very hard. This is known as the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) (RFC 6090, §§ 3.3.2, 4.2, and 10).

Security also depends on choosing good curve parameters. As stated in Section 3.3.2 of RFC 6090, the order of the subgroup generated by the base point must be divisible by a large prime number. This prevents attackers from finding easy ways to solve the discrete logarithm problem. The document also explains that bad parameter choices can make this problem much easier to solve: for instance, certain combinations of curve coefficients and finite field characteristics are considered weak for cryptography and should never be used (McGrew et al., 2011, § 3.3.2;).

In addition, we must separate this property from the properties of cryptographic hash functions. Hash functions are built to provide collision resistance, preimage resistance, and second preimage resistance, as well as a strong diffusion effect usually called the avalanche effect. Even though we can test some of these properties with statistical tools, these tests alone cannot prove that a system is secure.

How it works (Point Addition)

Each step in the chain adds G to the previous point. To add two points P = (x₁, y₁) and Q = (x₂, y₂):

Step 1 — Slope m:
Case A (Chord, P ≠ Q): m = (y₂ - y₁) ÷ (x₂ - x₁) mod 17
Case B (Doubling, P = Q): m = (3·x₁² + 0) ÷ (2·y₁) mod 17
Step 2 — New x:
x₃ = m² - x₁ - x₂ mod 17
Step 3 — New y:
y₃ = m·(x₁ - x₃) - y₁ mod 17

Point at Infinity (𝒪)

If P and Q share the same x but opposite y (i.e., y₁ + y₂ ≡ 0 mod 17), the chord is vertical.

The line never hits another affine point on the grid. The sum is therefore defined as the point at infinity, written as 𝒪.

• P + 𝒪 = P (Identity element)
• For G = (1, 5), 8G = (1, 12).
• 9G = 8G + G = (1, 12) + (1, 5) = 𝒪.
• The cyclic group order of G is therefore exactly n = 9.
